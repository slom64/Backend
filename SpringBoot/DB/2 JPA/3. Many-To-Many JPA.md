> In real-world projects, **splitting the Many-toMany relationship to two One-To-Many is almost always better.**

---

When you have a many-to-many relationship with extra columns on the join table (like a `timestamp`, `status`, or `quantity`), the standard `@ManyToMany` annotation is no longer sufficient. This is because `@ManyToMany` assumes the join table is a "hidden" entity managed entirely by JPA, containing only the two foreign keys.

To handle additional fields, you must promote the join table to a **full JPA Entity**.

You cannot put these extra columns in Class A or Class B because they don't belong to the individual objects; they belong to the **relationship** between them.

Instead of one `@ManyToMany` relationship, you split it into two `@OneToMany` relationships pointing to a new "Link" entity.
- **Class A** has a `@OneToMany` relationship with the **Link Entity**.
- **Class B** has a `@OneToMany` relationship with the **Link Entity**.
- **Link Entity** has `@ManyToOne` relationships with both A and B, plus your **extra columns**.

---

> [!Question]
> what is better in jpa, use Many-to-Many relationship or split it to two One-to-Many?
#### 1. Using `@ManyToMany` (The Direct Approach)

Use this when the relationship is "pure." For example, a `User` can have many `Roles`, and a `Role` can belong to many `Users`. You don't need to know _when_ they got the role or _who_ assigned it.
- **How it works:** JPA creates a hidden "Join Table" behind the scenes.
- **Pros:** Clean Java code; you can simply do `user.getRoles().add(role)`.
- **Cons:** You **cannot** add extra columns to the join table (like `assigned_date` or `priority`). If you decide later that you need a "Created At" timestamp on that link, you have to refactor the entire relationship.
#### 2. Splitting into Two `@OneToMany` (The Mapping Entity)
This involves creating a third entity (an "Association Class") that sits between your two main entities. For example, instead of `User <-> Product`, you have `User <-> OrderItem <-> Product`.
- **How it works:** You create an entity (e.g., `UserRole`) that has a `@ManyToOne` to `User` and a `@ManyToOne` to `Role`.
- **Pros:** 
	- **Flexibility:** you can add columns like `status`, `created_at`, or `price_at_time_of_purchase`
    - **Better Control:** It's easier to query the relationship directly.
- **Cons:** More "boilerplate" code; you have to manage the intermediate object manually.
